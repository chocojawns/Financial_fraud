# Fifth Third: when the keyword matches occurred and what was documented

This follow-up uses the original Debian 8-K run. No expanded 10-K/10-Q result
set has been uploaded as of this review. The original data remain unchanged.

## Match timeline

Fifth Third had **44 fraud-vocabulary-positive filings among 185 successfully
scanned 8-K documents**. The earliest positive in this scan was April 21, 2020;
the latest was January 29, 2026. These are filing dates, not fraud incident dates.
The scan begins in 2020 and cannot establish the first-ever public mention.

| Filing year | Scanned Fifth Third filings | Fraud-keyword filings | Keyword-positive share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2020 | 32 | 7 | 21.9% |
| 2021 | 31 | 11 | 35.5% |
| 2022 | 23 | 8 | 34.8% |
| 2023 | 23 | 4 | 17.4% |
| 2024 | 27 | 8 | 29.6% |
| 2025 | 23 | 4 | 17.4% |
| 2026 | 26 | 2 | 7.7% |

The largest count was 2021 (11 filings), followed by 2022 and 2024 (8 each).
Neither the counts nor percentages establish a rise/fall in actual fraud.
2026 is incomplete, and disclosure wording and filing purposes can change.

The saved excerpts break down into 37 filings with the exact risk phrase
“losses related to fraud, theft, misappropriation or violence,” five with the
earlier phrase “losses related to fraud, theft or violence,” and two with
COVID-related general risk warnings mentioning theft/fraud. These are excerpt
classifications, not proof the entire documents contain only boilerplate.

**Important:** the July 9, 2024 filing contains both the repeated warning and
substantive regulatory-action disclosures. A generic excerpt can therefore
mask an important event elsewhere in the same document.

## A documented regulatory matter: July 9, 2024

The full [July 9, 2024 Fifth Third 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/35527/000003552724000186/fitb-20240709.htm)
was retrieved directly from the SEC for this follow-up. Under **Item 8.01**,
it states that the CFPB filed a lawsuit on **March 9, 2020**, alleging violations
of consumer-financial laws. The bank disclosed the following resolutions:

| Matter | Disclosed agreement | Customer-redress scope stated in the filing |
|---|---|---|
| Account-opening/sales-practices lawsuit | $15 million civil monetary penalty; compliance and redress plans | Certain checking/savings/credit-card accounts opened January 1, 2010–December 31, 2016 |
| Discontinued auto-loan collateral-protection insurance program | $5 million civil monetary penalty; compliance and redress plans | Redress period July 21, 2011–December 31, 2020 |

The filing says the agreements were entered **without admitting or denying**
the specified allegations/findings, subject to the stated exceptions. It attaches
the judgment/order and consent order as exhibits. This review verified the primary
8-K text; it does not substitute for a full legal analysis of those orders.

The two disclosed penalties total **$20 million**, separate from customer redress.
The primary disclosure does not provide a total dollar figure for that redress.
The document establishes agreements to pay penalties, not proof of their later
payment dates. The redress periods are not necessarily complete misconduct dates.

This is financial evidence about regulatory exposure/alleged business conduct,
not proof the bank lost $20 million to outside fraud or that criminals earned it.
The resolution date is not the date every underlying action occurred.

## What this changes in the discovery analysis

Financials had 93 keyword-positive filings; Fifth Third contributed 44 (47.3%).
Repeated risk language explains much of the apparent concentration, but that
must not lead us to discard every such document. The July 2024 example shows
why whole-document context and financial/legal sections matter.

The next review should separate risk wording, alleged company misconduct,
regulatory resolutions, company victimization, and quantified financial impact.
A useful question is: **How often do fraud-related disclosures provide a specific
financial amount, and what kind of amount is it?** Fifth Third supplies a concrete
penalty example; Coinbase supplies a distinct remediation/reimbursement estimate.
They should not be pooled as identical fraud-loss measures.

No evidence here supports 44 independent fraud events, a causal link between the
2021 keyword peak and the 2024 resolution, or the claim about fraud being the
second-largest revenue generator.
